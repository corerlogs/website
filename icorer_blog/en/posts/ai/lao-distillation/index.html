Teach It Lao Without Losing Thai

Together distilled Qwen3.8-27B toward Kimi-K3.1-Pro on Lao: chrF++ went from 5.5 to 44.3, or 93% of the teacher. In the same batch of runs Thai fell to 5.3. It took 989 Thai rows to hold it. Every number here is from their post, none re-measured.

A 27B model that writes good Thai answered Lao requests in Thai, and got the meaning right. What it got wrong was which language it was in. A run that repairs the target language and wrecks the neighbour still wins a target-only leaderboard.

1. What a 5.5 looks like#

Together ran a batch of experiments in November 2026 and asked Qwen3.8-27B for Lao. That model is 27B parameters, one of the most widely used small models in its class, and it writes Thai well. Asked to translate English into Lao, it returned Thai.

The two languages are close. Both sit in the Southwestern Tai branch, sharing word order and a lot of vocabulary, with completely different scripts. It treated the two as one thing.

On 1,012 Lao sentences from FLORES-200 the model scored 5.5 chrF++, a character and word overlap count against a human reference, 0 to 100. Kimi-K3.1-Pro scored 47.5 on the same set.

Only 11% of its outputs were mostly in Lao script.

A 50-sentence screen shows the shape. 45 of 50 outputs carried Thai characters, 6 carried Lao. The translation itself is reasonable. It sits in the wrong alphabet.

2. It knew the sentence, not the language#

Have the teacher translate the student’s Lao back into English, then score that against the original. That is round-trip adequacy.

The base model’s Thai output scored 64.6, slightly above the human Lao reference at 64.0. The meaning was already there. After distillation the student scored 65.7 and wrote Lao on every test sentence.

So the gap was language identity, not semantics. It explains a second thing in the training logs. Every run fixed the script by its first evaluation, 25 or 50 steps in, and spent the rest of its steps on something else.

3. Why off-policy#

On-policy distillation needs the teacher’s log-probabilities on tokens the student produced. That means a shared tokenizer. Kimi and Qwen do not share one, so the road is closed.

Off-policy distillation needs text from the teacher and nothing else. Each teacher call is an ordinary serverless chat request, and a very large model never has to hold a GPU of its own.

The money goes to the teacher. Kimi-K3.1-Pro charges $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output. All 4,000 Lao translations cost $7.46. Every experiment in the post together cost $260, counted at B200 rates.

Thinking has to be off. With it on, the teacher scored 43.5 on the same 300 Lao sentences, against 47.3 with it off, and used about eight times the tokens. Translation does not need a long chain of reasoning.

Rows pass a filter before they enter training, at least 70% of characters in the target script and no echo of the English source. The filter dropped 4% of the teacher’s Lao.

4. 25 steps fix Lao and flatten Thai#

A control run trained on human references, 945 Lao sentences from FLORES dev, with no teacher involved. Lao went from 6.7 to 43.3 inside 25 steps.

Thai went the other way. It fell from 46.7 to 11.7, ended at 5.3, and 0% of Thai outputs were in Thai script. Not one of 300 sentences came back in Thai.

The model learned “write Lao when asked to translate”, not “write the language you were asked for”. The problem changed direction and stayed in place.

On the Lao column alone, that run had the highest student score in the post at 44.7. It would have shipped.

5. Add 989 Thai rows#

Distilling Lao alone, on the teacher’s translations of the same sentences, slowed the damage without stopping it. Thai held for two epochs, slipped to 44.3 in the third, and 17 of 300 Thai answers came back in Lao script.

About 1,000 teacher-translated Thai rows fixed it. Thai finished at 47.4 and 47.8 across the two mixed runs, above the base model’s 46.7, and never dropped below 47.3 at any evaluation.

6. The last 3 points, style and supply#

The best distilled student reached 44.3 against 44.7 for the human-reference student, a gap of 0.4. Two things sit behind it.

Reference style. FLORES dev and devtest came out of the same human translation process, so training on dev teaches the devtest house style early. Change the ruler and the ranking moves. Scored against the teacher’s own translations, the distilled student climbed from 8 to 58, and the human-reference student reached 47.

Supply. Dev holds 997 human references and no more. The teacher can translate any English you have. Adding 3,000 Wikipedia sentences took the training set from 965 rows to 3,834 and Lao from 41.9 to 44.3, with Thai intact.

Data beats passes. One epoch on 4× the data reached 43.2, against 41.3 for three epochs on the smaller set.

7. Five languages, one adapter#

To check this was not a Lao story, the three widest gaps plus Yoruba went into one adapter, with Thai along for retention. That is 8,871 teacher translations, about 2,000 per language and 1,000 for Thai, over 831 steps.

Every language moved up. Zulu moved least, and during training 1 to 2% of its outputs fell into repetition loops. More Zulu rows or a repetition penalty is the next thing to try.

8. Four things we would keep#

  • Screen first. Fifty sentences per language were enough to see where the teacher was far ahead.
  • Read the base model’s errors before training. Here the meaning was right and the language was wrong, which is why the script moved in 25 to 50 steps. A knowledge gap would not move in 25 steps.
  • When the student plateaus, add teacher data before you add epochs.
  • Mix in the nearby skill you want to keep, and score it during training. Score Lao alone and the run that broke Thai ships as the winner.

The appendix loss curves are worth a separate look. The human-reference run opened at 0.54 nats per trained token and the teacher-translation run at 0.36, ending at 0.16 and 0.06. One model’s translations are more self-consistent than a team of translators, so the student has less to fit.

Loss steps down at every epoch boundary while held-out chrF++ moves between -1 and +3 points. The step down is the model meeting rows it has already seen.

9. The ceiling, and two open questions#

The ceiling is the teacher. 47.5 was the top this time and the student reached 44.3, so a stronger teacher lifts the line with it. The student also inherits the teacher’s style, which means a distilled student can look worse than a human-reference student on a different evaluation, for reasons that live in the reference and not in the model.

Two questions I cannot answer. Why Zulu closes the least of the five, whether that is data volume, the repetition loops, or the language’s morphology.

And whether one adapter holding five languages interferes with itself. This run showed all five improving at once, with no single-language ablation.
