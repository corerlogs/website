SecLog, read closely: log the data handover, not the payment

Posted by LB on Mon, Nov 2, 2026
中文版是这篇文章的默认版本。 阅读中文版 →
This English page stays available at this URL; the Chinese version is the one we maintain first.

In one line: “the user cannot deny receiving the data” needs no trusted API and no data on chain — only a design where the last half of the decryption key can appear through one path, and that path leaves a record.

Just over two minutes: how a key becomes a log entry → the two events and what each one proves → the 8,000-gas gap, and why I could not reproduce the absolute number.

The paper is SecLog: Atomic-Swap Based Secure Logging for Decentralized Data Sharing (Hughes, Hossain, Tang, Jung, Chenli; METAVERSE 2025, LNCS 16159, pp. 115–124). Four pages, cleanly built: §1 states the problem, §2 sorts prior work into two lines and introduces the Web3DB substrate, §3 gives the model, the protocol and two theorems, §4 measures three contracts and discusses the cost, §5 closes. What follows walks that order, marking which section each claim comes from, and ends with our own rerun.

1. The problem: after you sign the consent form

The paper opens on a concrete scene: data is generated every day and usually collected and shared by central parties — companies, hospitals, data centres; that sharing improves products and research, while the users who generate the data barely take part in it:

“In many cases, the only thing that data generators … know about this process is when they initially sign the agreement for allowing some entity to share their data.” — §1

After signature the owner knows nothing: who accessed the data, when, and how it was used. That is not a UX complaint — sharing without transparency is already non-compliant with HIPAA, GDPR and CCPA.

Decentralized databases (the paper points at recallnet’s awesome-decentralized-database list) hand ownership and control back to the owner. But once ownership returns, a hole opens: nobody keeps the record. The paper splits that hole into three concrete failures:

The naive fixWhy it fails (paper §1)
Ask the user to confirm receipt“users who want to deny/repudiate the receipt of the retrieved data … will not confirm with the API”
Let the API write the log alonea network problem can leave the user genuinely empty-handed, so the log turns false; and the API becomes a single point of failure
Put the sharing events straight on a blockchainthe paper’s own words: cannot guarantee the correctness and the completeness of the logging — a chain gives you immutability, not “the entry that should exist, exists”

That third point is the crux. What this setting lacks is not tamper-resistance but enforcement: the record must be impossible to skip. So the paper borrows from fair exchange, where HTLC-style atomic swaps have bound “access to the data” and “payment” into one all-or-nothing step. SecLog’s insight fits in a sentence — swap the payment for the record and atomicity survives — because in this setting the user never pays the API for a retrieval anyway; what needs to be atomic is the data arriving and an event happening.

Four contributions, as listed: (1) an atomic-swap based logging protocol that enforces a record whenever a sharing succeeds in a decentralized setting; (2) a security proof; (3) a simulation showing lower gas than payment-based atomic swaps; (4) open code at github.com/avocado-avery/SecLog.

2. Two research lines, and the substrate they sit on

§2 splits into three subsections that map exactly onto the figure below — two lines and one substrate:

§2.1 Blockchain logging. Gao uses homomorphic encryption for audit logs on chain — confidentiality up, at the price of excluding users from verifying inclusion. Cha’s IoT auditing system leans on trusted infrastructure. Zhao and López-Pimentel store Merkle hashes and roots on chain for tamper evidence, with the detail off chain, which is where availability becomes a worry. Hsu combines blockchain with access control for IoT storage; Cha and Hasan use trusted execution environments for cloud logs. AuditChain, BlockAuditor and LogChain adopt more decentralized designs but still assume honest participants in the infrastructure and do not address omission attacks, where events are silently excluded. The paper’s summary of the line is worth quoting:

“…the absence of mechanisms that allow users to independently confirm that their actions were successfully logged.” — §2.1

SecLog’s placement is to fold verification into the logging process itself: enabling verifiable inclusion and censorship resistance without requiring trusted logger, storage node, or any single participant (ibid).

§2.2 Fair exchange. FairSwap, OptiSwap and Universal Atomic Swaps enforce atomicity through contracts and cryptographic primitives; Zhu and Tas push it into private-key exchange and zero-knowledge proofs. Their shared boundary is scope: they are built for a single-shot trade — coins for a file. The paper’s verdict: their models do not support persistent, auditable event streams, so they cannot carry “log every query”.

§2.3 The substrate, Web3DB. A decentralized RDBMS that answers SQL and hands control back to the data owner, built from four layers — data injection, access control, database engine, and storage on IPFS.

A query goes to the SQL API; the API asks the access control layer to validate credentials and rewrite the query against the ACL; the engine layer answers, with a randomly selected master node coordinating the slaves; the result returns to the API and then to the user. And here the paper names the screw that SecLog will loosen:

“However, the underlying assumption behind this process is that the users are honest, i.e., they will not deny receiving the results.” — §3.1

Workable on a hospital LAN. Untenable on the Internet.

3. System model, security model, three assumptions

§3.1 keeps its scope deliberately narrow: three kinds of entity — users (send queries, wait for results), the API (receives, collects, returns), and blockchain nodes (validate transactions, execute contracts). Node misbehaviour is explicitly out of scope: that belongs to consensus research, not to this paper.

The security model is two one-line definitions:

  • Correctness (Definition 1) — every sharing record on chain corresponds to a sharing that actually finished: the API sent the result and the user received the data.
  • Completeness (Definition 2) — every successful retrieval leaves a corresponding record on chain.

Those two are the whole target. Three assumptions are what holds them up:

  1. The chain is secure and not selectively censoring: no 51% attack, no collusion to censor specific transactions (the user’s deployment, the API’s call), so liveness is guaranteed — as long as the user gets online once after the API’s response is confirmed, they will eventually have the data.
  2. Payloads travel encrypted: otherwise the data is readable by every node, or the querier can deny having received it.
  3. The API crashes, never lies: crash faults only (worst case, unresponsive); no Byzantine faults, and a malicious data provider is out of scope — data correctness is Fair²Trade’s line of work.

4. The design: four steps, one point, two events

§3.2’s four steps read well next to the figure:

  1. The API encrypts the result with its own key sk, then splits that key at random: sk = sk1 + sk2. Both steps are off-chain.

  2. It sends sk1 and the ciphertext to the front-end user. So far sk1 is known only to the API and the user.

  3. The user derives a condition value from sk1 and deploys a contract that carries it. With the paper’s ElGamal example, the public key is pk = g^sk, so

    1cond = pk / g^sk1 = g^sk / g^sk1 = g^(sk − sk1) = g^sk2

    The contract is a variant HTLC: only an input satisfying ecMul(sk2, G) == cond (i.e. g^input == cond) fires and records the receipt event; otherwise nothing is logged and the contract lapses at its expiry.

  4. The API checks that cond is the point it expects. If yes, it calls the contract with sk2, which fires the logging event; if not, it ignores the contract.

Input verification is not new work: the paper says it follows Fair²Trade’s implementation (ElGamal plus an elliptic-curve multiply), and that the contribution here is enforcement of the log rather than the verification primitive, so the full construction and its security analysis live in the earlier paper.

The contract is 4.2 KB. One naming trap before reading it: the fields are called sk1x / sk1y but hold the point g^sk2 — the user’s commitment — not sk1.

1// contracts/SecLog.sol: sklockMatches is the whole paper's atomicity
2(sk2x, sk2y) = EllipticCurve.ecMul(sk2, GX, GY, AA, PP);      // sk2 * G
3require(logs[_logId].sk1x == sk2x, "sk2 does not match");     // compare with the user's point
4require(logs[_logId].sk1y == sk2y, "sk2 does not match");

1// contracts/SecLog.sol: only the receiver (the API) may trigger it, and only once
2require(log.receiver == msg.sender, "Only receiver can verify");
3require(keccak256(_message) == log.messageHash, "Message hash mismatch");
4log.verified = true;
5emit LogVerified(_logId);

The two events are the skeleton of the paper:

  • LogEntryNew (emitted when the user deploys): logId, both addresses, the commitment point, messageHash, timelock. It proves intent.
  • LogVerified (emitted when the API calls verifyLog): only fires when the point and the message hash match. It proves fact, and it writes sk2 permanently into calldata.

5. Security analysis: two theorems, one paragraph each

§3.3 is brief by design.

Theorem 1 (correctness). The design guarantees correctness as long as the user can access the blockchain at least once after the API’s response is confirmed. Proof. With stable network access the user recovers sk as soon as the API provides the correct sk2. The special case is a user who is offline and cannot see chain updates in time; by the §3.1 assumption the chain is live, so once the API’s input is confirmed and the user connects even once, they read sk2 and recover the result.

Theorem 2 (completeness). The design guarantees completeness as long as the API provides a valid input to a valid contract deployed by the user. Proof. By the atomic property of the HTLC: when the correct input is provided, chain nodes validate it and execute the logging event. So as long as the contract is deployed correctly, a valid input always fires the event.

Both proofs hold, and both are short — because they name what the model already granted. The real work lives in §3.1’s three assumptions: liveness, encryption, and an API that does not lie. Which is the sentence we most want to add to this paper: its security is bought with the liveness of a chain; it moves trust from an API to a whole chain rather than removing it.

6. Evaluation: three contracts, one chart, and our rerun

§4.1’s setup: Ethereum smart contracts, Truffle with a local Ganache chain, Solidity 0.5.0, gas read straight from Ganache logs and the Truffle console. Three contracts:

  • SecLog.sol — this paper.
  • Fair2Trade.sol — the payment-based baseline. In Fair²Trade the data buyer sets up an HTLC and the data broker redeems the deposit by handing over access.
  • CIDLog.sol — a naive baseline the authors wrote for comparison: don’t encrypt, store the result on IPFS, and use the result’s CID as the sk in the HTLC.

The headline: SecLog costs about 8,000 gas less than Fair²Trade. The reason is that Fair²Trade launches a deposit transfer once the broker provides a valid sk2, while SecLog only fires a “valid input received” event. The gap is smaller than an ETH transfer’s 21,000 gas because SecLog’s extra confirmation step also costs something. CIDLog lands on the same gas as SecLog — a CID is 32 bytes like sk, so verification costs the same — but a CIDLog user cannot download anything until the swap completes, and the design drags in one more entity (an IPFS node) and a harder verification problem.

We put the same bytecode on a local EVM (solc 0.5.17, py-evm on the Istanbul and London forks). Half of it reproduces, half does not:

  • The relative claim reproduces: Fair2Trade − SecLog = 8,038 gas on London, 8,026 on Istanbul. The paper’s prose says 8,000; the authors’ chart data says 8,166 (696,991 − 688,825). Within 1.6%.
  • The absolute number does not: Fig. 2 reports 688,825 gas; the same bytecode costs 186,415 gas here — 3.7×. I did suspect the EVM fork: the Berlin modexp repricing is large, and this curve library does modular inversion through modexp. But Istanbul gives 179,327, still nowhere near, so that explanation dies. A four-page paper measured on Truffle + Ganache is a different stack — gas absolutes do not travel between papers; the delta and the order of magnitude do.

The paper also leaves one anchor anyone can recompute: per Etherscan’s gas tracker on 2025-07-26, one execution costs about $0.75. At that same gas price, our 186,415 gas is about $0.20 — and the user’s deployment (1,164,022 gas) is about $1.27.

7. Discussion: the three costs the paper admits

§4.2 is the most honest page of the paper. It lists three kinds of added cost without dressing them up:

  1. Network burden. Users must interact with the chain to receive the API’s input, which raises the bar on their connectivity; and the sync frequency depends on the underlying chain — Bitcoin blocks are ~10 minutes, Ethereum’s ~12 seconds.
  2. Chain maintenance. Liveness is an assumption, and the cost of maintaining the chain is not in the protocol’s ledger; which chain you pick is an implementation decision, and the maintenance cost moves with it.
  3. Off-chain overhead. Encryption, decryption and key splitting. The paper’s magnitudes: AES on a 1 MB payload takes under 10 ms, key splitting under 1 ms — both small next to the on-chain cost.

Put those three on the same ruler as §4.1’s gas and you get this:

The reading is sharper than the paper’s own text: cryptography is nearly free next to consensus. The cost of this protocol is not the elliptic curve, it is waiting for a block and paying for one. So “8,000 gas cheaper than the payment-based swap” is true, and it is one small cell in a much larger bill whose first line is a 1.16M-gas deployment.

That covers half the picture. Draw the other half as a matrix — what the design closes and what it explicitly does not — and the boundary is easy to hold in mind:

9. What you can check yourself, and the two questions the paper leaves

Three commands, all against public data, no chain required:

 1# 1. the atomicity line: what the contract actually compares
 2curl -s https://raw.githubusercontent.com/avocado-avery/SecLog/main/contracts/SecLog.sol \
 3  | grep -n "ecMul\|keccak256(_message)\|emit LogVerified"
 4
 5# 2. is the query public? look at verifyLog's parameters
 6curl -s https://raw.githubusercontent.com/avocado-avery/SecLog/main/contracts/SecLog.sol \
 7  | grep -n "function verifyLog" -A 3
 8
 9# 3. cost: back out the implied gas price from "688,825 gas ≈ $0.75", then price the rerun
10python3 -c "p=0.75/688825; print(round(p*1e9,3),'gwei-equivalent'); print('186,415 gas ->', round(186415*p,2), 'USD')"

The second one is the load-bearing check: verifyLog(logId, sk2, message) needs message in the clear, otherwise keccak256(_message) cannot match messageHash. In other words, the design assumes the query is published alongside the record. Keep privacy and you must replace messageHash with a commitment (a hash of a hash) — and then the contract can no longer check the content. That is a fork in the road the paper does not take.

Two questions left open:

  • Who pays the bill? The paper compares only the verification step. Total the exchange: the user fronts ~1.16M gas to deploy, the API pays ~186k to verify, while the naive “API writes one log entry” needs one SSTORE and one event. What the 8,000 gas buys is not trusting the API. In how many settings is that trust gap worth a million gas? The paper does not say.
  • Can it aggregate? One transaction per query means 10,000 transactions for 10,000 queries. The authors’ other Fair²Trade line amortises with epoch + Merkle roots (their Chart.ipynb is literally about this); SecLog does not. Replace the single curve commitment with a Merkle tree — does atomicity survive?

I cannot answer either. The second looks like the obvious next paper.
