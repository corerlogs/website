The one-person creative team, and the bill behind the pitch

Dan Koe's long letter argues that capturing attention has always been the most lucrative skill. I checked its three numbers, took apart its three jobs, and found one thing worth keeping inside the sales pitch.

In one line: attention is finite, so capturing it is a real craft and a real trade. What sells is the work of deciding who someone is, not thirty posts a day.

On a November evening I read a long letter. Dan Koe wrote it, and the subject was how to capture attention on social platforms. It ran long enough that I got up twice for hot water, and by the second trip the tea had stopped being hot.

It opens with the conclusion. The most lucrative skill, he writes, has always been capturing attention. Two screens down there is a registration link, forty-nine dollars, a workshop on writing. I do not know the man and I had no intention of signing up, and I read the whole thing anyway. He was describing work I do every day from the opposite direction. I write, and I try not to interrupt people.

What follows is my reading of it. Every number carries its origin. His own claims are marked as his, and they do not share a table row with anything published.

1. Three roads, and only one has traffic#

His argument starts with job security, which he thinks is going away. That leaves three options. Complain about how unfair things are and do nothing. Learn a new trade and find a better job. Start the business you have been carrying around in your head.

He expects the third road to fill up. I believe him. The first two are undignified. Ten years of complaining leaves you where you started, and learning a trade requires sitting still, which is harder than opening a shop.

The first step of opening a shop, he says, is building a personal brand. When I first met that phrase I pictured business cards. A card is paper. A personal brand is the noise you leave in the world, and the two are not close relatives.

I thought about opening a shop when I was younger. I did the sums. Rent costs money, stock costs money, and the part that ruins the plan is that somebody has to walk in. A personal brand makes the rent free. It does nothing about the walk-in.

2. Three numbers about the market, none about you#

The letter makes its case with three figures. I copied them down and marked each one.

MetricValueSourceBasis
Creator economy, total$250B (2023) to $480B (2027)Goldman Sachs Research, 2023-04forecast
US brand spend on creators$13.9B (2021) to $43.9B (2026)IABactual plus forecast
Creator ad revenueabout $185B (2025)WPP Mediaforecast

All three are real, and together they say something true about where the money is moving. They say nothing about whether any of it arrives in your account.

I have watched a bull market. People lose money in those too, and they lose it for a decent reason. They bought at the top. Market size is one axis and your income is another, and the road between them runs past platform cuts, a crowded field, and an algorithm that changes its temper every few months.

The letter turns the IAB figure into three times in five years. Both endpoints check out. Five years is the safer word for it than three, because the last year has not arrived. A man writing a sales letter rounds in his own favour there. I do not hold it against him. Melon sellers praise melons.

3. The most honest sentence in the letter#

The line I believe most is this one. You can always make more money, and you will never get back the attention you spent.

It needs no citation. An afternoon has a fixed length. Give it to a course seller and it is not available for the Wanli Emperor, or for the book about him, or for the walk you did not take.

That same sentence is where the letter is soft. He argues that attention is finite while spending ten thousand words of yours. It is funny, and it is not quite dishonest, because he does not hide the trade. The best thing about the letter is that he counts himself in the bill.

One note for readers of this blog, most of whom build infrastructure. In that market attention is not the only variable. The person signing the purchase order wants metrics, integration cost, and a name to call at 3 a.m. One clear performance comparison does more work than thirty daily posts, because the comparison can be forwarded to a colleague who signs things.

4. One person as a creative team, which is also called overtime#

Peel off the sales layer and a job description is left.

Brand strategy owns position. Its main task is deciding what not to chase, and it answers the question of who you are and whose problem you take seriously.

Content strategy owns the calendar. This job watches what travels, which titles hold, which opening five seconds keep people in the frame.

Writing and operations turn the other two into posts, scripts, essays, and publish them on time.

In a real company those are three salaries. Folded into one person they get a nicer name, the one-person creative team, and an older one, overtime. The only question that matters is whether anyone pays for the three jobs.

There is a passage about AI that says revision went from twelve hours a day to four. No data comes with it, so I read it as advertising. The direction still feels right. Producing text got cheap. Deciding which version deserves to exist did not. Spend the recovered eight hours generating more drafts and you have only made the rubbish faster.

5. Of nine questions, the sixth is the hard one#

The letter includes an interview list, nine groups of questions, meant to turn a person into material an AI can read again and again. Who you help. Your story. Who shaped your mind. Content pillars. Mission and beliefs. What you are against. Why now. Where you are taking people. Your rules and your private vocabulary.

The first group is precise. It asks for one real person, the shape of that person’s bad day, the words they use when complaining to a friend. Vagueness does not survive that kind of question.

The sixth group is the shortest and the meanest. What are you against.

A person who cannot answer it writes minutes of a meeting. Minutes get written all the time and read by nobody, because they offend no one and leave nothing behind.

I am against very little, and what I am against most is the sentence everybody is doing it. Once that sentence is out, the argument behind it no longer has to be made.

The ninth group is the one people skip. It asks you to name your own method. Naming looks vain and turns out to be practical. A name is luggage, and whether a reader can carry your idea home depends on whether the luggage has a handle.

6. A content map is a publishing schedule#

With the material in hand, the next step is a calendar. The multiplication in the letter runs like this. Pick three or four pillars. Each splits into five to seven topics. Each topic yields five to ten ideas, and each idea carries four or five angles.

I did the arithmetic. That comes to about four hundred and eighty topics, or six months at one a day. Then I did my own arithmetic at three a day, which is five hundred and forty, and the number frightened me. I do not read that many books in a year.

I made schedules when I was young. A reading list on the wall, promising, and a year later the paper had yellowed and none of the books were finished. A reading list is for yourself. A schedule is for other people. When someone is waiting for Thursday, Thursday becomes real.

That difference is worth keeping. Discipline sounds like a strong word, and most of the time it is just somebody expecting you.

7. Ten free posts, then the price list#

The last concrete move in the letter comes from his friend JK Molina. Pick someone you want to work with, write ten posts for them for free, send the finished work with one line. Use them or send me a note.

He also gives a public version. Someone made a short video that opened with Grant Cardone needing a new creative director, and the observation that his content seemed stuck in 2016. Cardone replied. The reply was, hit me I’m stuck.

The move is old. Give a taste before you ask for money, which is how melons have been sold for a long time. Whether the writing is sweet depends on whose mouth is tasting, so the method has two preconditions. Pick the right person, and do not write badly. Ten bad drafts delivered together is ten public embarrassments.

He gives a price range as well. Two and a half to five thousand dollars a month to start, ten to seventy-five thousand a month at the top, serving one to three clients.

I believe that some people make a great deal of money from writing. I do not believe that writers make a great deal of money. One word separates those sentences and the word is carrying all the weight.

8. What the letter is, and what survives the discount#

This is a sales letter. The author founded Eden, and the letter promotes the tool and the forty-nine-dollar workshop. Putting that on the table first does not cancel the useful middle of it. It does tell you how to hold the rest.

The three jobs, used as a checklistThe price range, which is a ceiling and not an entry feeTime spent choosing topics every week
The nine questions, which force vagueness outDaily publishing, which costs an engineering team more than it returnsAttention cut into small pieces
Ten free posts, which move the cost of persuasion to youThe premise that attention is the only variableWriting that pleases instead of writing that holds

I wrote a second piece on the same letter with an engineer’s ruler, and it checks the numbers more closely. The link is at the end. This one reads the letter for its worldly wisdom.

9. What I kept#

That night I poured out the cold tea and made another cup. The next morning I went to work as usual, and attention went back to the people who pay for it.

The letter’s lesson fits in a sentence. The sign hangs outside, and the shop is the part you open yourself. A sign is for other people. The shop is the room you sweep every morning. Some people build only the sign. Two years later the sign is still there, and it rattles in the wind, and the room behind it is empty.

I have no plans to build a personal brand. My readers arrive slowly and leave slowly, and the way I talk to them is to write something they can check, then leave it where it is. The method is slow, and it does not require me to appear in front of anyone three times a day.

If one sentence from the letter goes on a card above my desk, it is the sixth question. A person who can say clearly what he is against will write something decent most of the time.
