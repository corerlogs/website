Machine Hermeneutics: the building that explains what the machine found

Jack Clark wrote a 2033 memoir about the day explanation became a discipline of its own. On this year's arXiv that discipline is already at work, and it has no name yet.

In one line: once machines do science faster than people can read it, explanation becomes a line of work with its own ledger. This year’s ledger can already be counted, and three different things are still being asked to justify it.

1. Two hundred and seventy-one rows on a screen#

Half of my screen holds a tracker page kept by a mathematician. It records the mathematics on arXiv this year that machines wrote, and the papers that follow them. By 25 November it listed 1,170 machine-generated works, with 274 arXiv papers following them up. Ninety-two of those appeared in November, and 72 of them follow the batch OpenAI released on 6 October.

Scroll down and the titles start to read like a different trade.

  • “Human Exposition of Claude’s 2/3 Theorem on the zeros of zeta”, 17 October

  • “Proof of the Harary-Hill conjecture: an exposition”, 25 October

  • “The satisfiability conjecture for random k-SAT formulas. Human-friendly exposition of the recent OpenAI resolution”, 6 November

  • “Notes on Kozma and Nitzan’s proof”, 7 October, described as a shorter proof written again by a person

“Human exposition.” That phrase did not use to appear in titles, because papers used to be written for humans by default.

Two things happened in the same week. On 20 November the tracker gained an entry that is not on arXiv. Levent Alpöge and Claude announced a proof of the Riemann hypothesis and of the generalised Riemann hypothesis for Dirichlet L-functions, delivered as the first of fourteen Lean archives. Five days later a paper on bounds for the tails of Dirichlet L-functions took that announcement as its only input on zeros. On 27 November Jack Clark posted a short story on X, “Machine architecture and the objectivity crisis of Machine Hermeneutics”. Eight hundred and thirty-three words, thirteen paragraphs, written as the memoir of an investigator in 2033.

A claimed Riemann hypothesis on one side, a building that does not exist yet on the other. I read the story three times. The distance between those two things is the subject.

2. A chain a person has to walk from end to end#

The story’s world has a law called the Sentience Accords. Any new insight or piece of equipment designed by a machine has to be fully explainable by humans before machines are allowed to use it. The procedure is called conceptual verification.

In the early years it was cheap. Machines did the science and handed the results to the world, people read them at their own pace, understood them, wrote an explanation another person could read, and then the thing could be used. Verification was a queue, and a queue takes time.

Then the chain grew links. A machine invents an idea, uses the idea to design a new instrument, and uses the instrument to make a discovery. To keep up, a person has to understand each link, and then understand the chain.

Explaining things became a discipline. Machine hermeneutics took shape in the mid-2020s. By the 2030s the highest achievement available to a human scientist is neither the breakthrough nor the command of the machine that makes it. It is an elegant explanation of the machine’s frontier that another human can learn from, and use to draw a map of the new territory.

Then come the didactic buildings. Structures built for machine hermeneutics experts, so that a concept the machines produced has somewhere a person can walk into. The first ones were crude, little more than 3D-printed extrusions inside ordinary flats. They worked, measurably. Cohorts that went through a building produced a human-readable explanation faster than cohorts that did not. The metric is TTHUE, time to a human-understandable explanation. A metric invites a race, and some people made building the buildings their main work.

The buildings are the best writing in the story. Investigators have been electrons, appearing and disappearing inside a 3D transistor. They have been qubits, placed in spheres that roll until the position resolves, up or down, at the moment the answer arrives. They have put their hands on surfaces that suggest the texture of a new heat shield. One corridor is six miles long and shaped like the path a plasma might take through a fusion reactor, and instead of magnets there is wind from all sides, holding the walker in the middle of the path.

3. What can already be counted this year#

Put the story down and read the tracker’s own numbers.

The list holds 1,170 machine-generated works and 274 follow-up papers. Ninety-two of the follow-ups appeared in the first twenty-five days of November, 72 of them on OpenAI’s 6 October release. September had 29, October had 121. New papers per month across 28 mathematics categories run at 8,246 in September, 8,739 in October and 7,255 in November to the 25th.

I counted the same page myself, taking only titles and descriptions that carry the words “exposition”, “expository” or “human-friendly”, and de-duplicating. Of 271 listed follow-up papers, 27 qualify. About one in ten. That tenth does not push the frontier forward. It explains what somebody else pushed forward.

One in ten looks small. The faster the machines produce, the more often the explaining has to be done, and when the explaining falls behind, more follow-up papers turn into explanations. The ratio does not fall on its own.

The ledger has a second face. Of the 1,170 machine-generated works, 137 have anything following them up, a bit over a tenth. The other eight hundred sit there. Some were read, some were not, some were half-read and abandoned. The tracker cannot tell the difference and I will not pretend to.

4. The half of the title that is missing#

The headline on X is half a sentence longer than the story. It adds “the objectivity crisis”. Those words do not appear in the story. The buildings stand, the metric runs, people do understand. Where the crisis lives is left for the reader to supply.

My attempt at supplying it starts with a question. Who decides that an explanation counts.

The Sentience Accords decide that a human must be able to produce an explanation. They do not say the explanation must be right. TTHUE measures how long it takes to reach a clear account, and where the account went is not the metric’s business. That is not an oversight in the story. The story is about a thing that has not been settled.

Three candidates are available today, and they are not judging the same object.

  • A formal checker such as Lean. It judges whether a formal object follows the rules to the end.

  • Peer review. It judges whether a few people are willing to put their names under it.

  • Teaching. It judges whether another person can do something with it.

The three disagree. A paper posted on 6 October, “Navier-Stokes lost in translation”, compares the natural-language proof of one piece of work with its Lean formalisation and argues that the two do not correspond. It explicitly makes no claim about the proof itself. The formalisation passed. Whether the sentences mean anything is a separate question.

5. Three judges, three verdicts#

The formal checker is the fastest and the narrowest. It is the only one of the three that a machine can certify by itself, so it never queues. It cannot help with translation. A line in a paper that says “by symmetry” may be a hundred lemmas in a formal proof, or it may be nothing at all.

Peer review is the slow one. There are 8,739 new papers in a month spread over 28 categories. Refereeing is people reading other people’s work after hours, and that has a ceiling. What has grown fastest over the past eighteen months is exactly the material waiting to be read. arXiv mathematics grew at roughly nine percent a year for three decades, with two breaks in the growth rate after the pandemic. The paper that studies those breaks had its data collection, analysis and first draft produced by an agent.

Teaching is the slowest of the three, and the only judge that leaves a person able to work. Twenty-seven explanations sit on that list, each one written by somebody who actually got through the paper.

The other side deserves a hearing. A November paper, “The Industrialization of Noise”, brings back an argument DeMillo, Lipton and Perlis made in 1979. A proof was never a static structure of formal logic. It is a social process of criticism, internalisation and shared intuition. OpenAI released 722 automatically generated mathematical manuscripts in October, claiming full or partial solutions to 372 longstanding open problems. The paper argues that pulling solving away from understanding leaves a warehouse of logical artefacts nobody has read. Posting a result and submitting it to the community are two different acts.

I nodded through the first half and then found it too tidy. Twenty-seven explanations sit on that list, which means the social process did not stop. It moved, from checking to teaching.

6. Why a building can hold a concept#

Back to the buildings. Why does walking through one save time.

Because some concepts have no sentences yet. An electron in a transistor, a qubit whose position is undecided, the bend a plasma takes inside a reactor. All of them can be written as equations, and equations give no sense of direction. A building gives direction. Which stretch is long, which one blocks, which one has to be walked around. A person walks it once, keeps it in the body, and afterwards the equation has somewhere to hang.

This is not a new trade. The metal model of the double helix, Feynman diagrams, the cutaway steam engine in a museum. All of them turn something invisible into something you can look at. The old models were made after the concept had been explained. The buildings are the raw material for the explanation. One is a product, the other is a step in the process.

The cost is in the story, near the end. A group gathers for a plain dinner at a low table and finds itself whispering although nobody asked for quiet. It begins to feel as if the building is teaching them something beyond the concepts, something about the minds that produced the concepts. Effort spent understanding what the machines made slowly reshapes the people doing the understanding into something closer to the machines.

The metric has a hole of its own. A building can be optimised for TTHUE. Arriving at the feeling of understanding and arriving at understanding register as the same number.

Now do the arithmetic per person. 274 follow-up papers at one reader-day each, over 250 working days a year, comes to a year and a month. The 1,170 machine-generated works at the same rate come to four years and eight months. Nobody actually books a year of reading like that. The sum is here to fix a magnitude.

How long the machines took is not published. Clark’s line is tens to hundreds of times less. I repeat it without a way to check it.

The most expensive item in the bill cannot be bought in bulk. One explanation can teach many people, and the person who writes it had to understand first. Reading is one person at a time. Explaining can be one to many. The faster the machines get, the further upstream the bottleneck moves, until it sits on whoever read the paper first.

8. A forty-five minute check#

If you want your own TTHUE reading, forty-five minutes will do.

Take one paper from the tracker with “exposition” in the title. Read only the abstract, close it, and write one paragraph stating the main theorem. Then read the introduction and count the places where your paragraph was wrong. Time it. If nothing on the list appeals, use the satisfiability exposition from 6 November.

Two numbers together give the word its weight. Forty-five minutes and three errors is roughly what one human-understandable explanation costs in you.

I cannot read 271 rows myself. Anyone can take one row, and once it is taken that row stops being somebody else’s number.

9. Two questions I cannot answer#

The first. If the test is that another person can repeat the explanation, where did the first person’s understanding come from. The story walks around that question and hands out buildings and a metric instead. I have no answer either. I only know that the longer the queue, the more expensive the first person’s seat becomes.

The second. If the explainers walk through buildings for years and their thinking changes shape, is their explanation still an independent verdict. That is the last paragraph of the story, and it stops there, with dinner still on the table and nobody replying.

I did one small thing. Twenty-seven titles went into a table, one paper a week, one paragraph after each. The weeks I cannot write a paragraph, the row stays empty.
